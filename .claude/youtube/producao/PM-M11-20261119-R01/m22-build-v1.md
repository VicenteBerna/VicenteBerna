## F. FULL NUMBERED NARRATION — v1

**01.** This spring, AI was the number one reason American companies gave for cutting jobs. By October, it had almost disappeared from their announcements. The technology didn't change that fast. The story did. And your risk was never in the story.

**02.** Over the next few minutes, we'll look at what the numbers actually show, why the reason a company gives for a layoff is a message rather than a measurement, and how to judge your own exposure without waiting for the next headline. One boundary first. These figures count job cuts that U.S. employers announced, and the reasons those employers chose to state. They are not a census of every layoff, and they don't measure how many jobs AI has actually replaced. That limit isn't a footnote. It's the point.

**03.** The numbers come from Challenger, Gray and Christmas, a firm that tracks announced job cuts every month and began tracking AI as a separate reason in 2023. In January of this year, AI was cited in seven percent of announced cuts. By March, twenty-five percent. Then came May. Employers cited AI for 38,579 announced job cuts — forty percent of everything announced that month. It was the highest monthly total since the firm started counting.

**04.** Then the line bent the other way. By the fall, companies were pointing somewhere else. In October, AI was cited for just 1,265 cuts — its lowest month of the year, and less than three percent of everything announced. The top reason became the economy itself. Market and economic conditions led October, and for the year, they have now overtaken AI as the leading reason companies give.

**05.** So what happened between May and October? Did artificial intelligence suddenly stop being useful? Did companies finish automating everything they planned to automate in a single summer? Neither explanation fits well. Inside a company, technology moves through budgets, pilots, contracts and training. It doesn't rise and fall in five months. Something else moved faster.

**06.** Here is what moved. A layoff announcement is not a lab report. It is a message, and it has several audiences at once — investors, employees, customers, and increasingly, regulators. The firm that tracks these numbers said it plainly this summer: naming AI in a layoff announcement can win over investors while pushing current and prospective employees away. That is an observation from the people who collect the data, not proof about any single company. But it tells you how to read the label.

**07.** Even the counting has edges. This October, Microsoft offered voluntary exits to sixty thousand employees. None of those offers appear in October's total, because the tracker only counts a voluntary program when people accept it, or when a company states a target. The tracker even keeps a separate category for cuts where new technology is named but AI is only implied — technological update, possibly AI. What gets counted, and what gets called a reason, is always a choice someone made.

**08.** And here is the contradiction worth holding onto. Even after the fall, AI was named in about one in five announced cuts this year — 121,401 of them, more than double the 54,836 in all of 2025. Yet total announced cuts fell forty-four percent compared with last year. More AI in the explanations. Fewer cuts overall. Which means the question that matters was never whether AI is taking jobs. It's something much closer to home.

**09.** If you work in an office, you may have felt both sides of this year. In the spring, the headlines said AI was coming for white-collar work, and it was hard not to wonder whether your experience still counted. By the fall, the headlines moved on, and it was easy to relax. Fear, then relief. Both reactions share one flaw. Neither one was based on your own work.

**10.** That's the reframe. AI won't replace everyone. But it will expose everyone. It exposes which parts of a job follow a pattern and which depend on judgment. And it exposes who has built options, and who has only built a position. Even the firm counting these cuts put it this way: AI is shifting the labor market — it is not dismantling it. A shift is exactly when the difference between a position and an option starts to matter.

**11.** The market around you makes that difference sharper. The same firm describes this as a low-fire environment. Employers are holding on to the workers they have. But most of the hiring they've announced this year is seasonal — about seventy-two percent of all hiring plans. A market that rarely fires and rarely hires feels safe while you stay. It gets harder the moment you need to move.

**12.** So instead of reading the next headline, measure three things that actually belong to you.

**13.** First, exposure. Look at a normal week of your work and separate the tasks from the judgment. Tasks follow a pattern and can be described step by step. Judgment is where you weigh context, people and trade-offs. The larger the share of your week that is pattern, the more seriously you should take the shift — not as a reason to panic, but as a reason to move your effort toward the part of the work that requires you. One simple test: for two weeks, at the end of each day, write down one decision that only you could have made. If that list stays short, that is your signal.

**14.** Second, visibility. Inside your company, people remember the problems you solved. Outside, nobody does. Could you show, in a few sentences and with real examples, what you have done and why it mattered? Value that only your current employer can see is a form of dependence. Value you can show is an option. Keep a simple evidence file — the problem, what you did, and the result you can honestly describe — and update it after every project, not when you suddenly need a job.

**15.** Third, time. If your role changed next quarter, how many months could you take to choose your next step, instead of accepting the first offer that appears? That number is not about wealth. It's about whether a decision stays yours. Count it plainly: what you have set aside, divided by what your household really spends in a month. Not your salary — your expenses. Every month of runway is a month in which you are choosing, not reacting.

**16.** That's what the AI layoff story really reveals. The reason printed in an announcement tells you what a company wanted to say that month. It doesn't tell you your risk. Your risk lives in three places the headlines never measure: how exposed your work is, how visible your value is, and how much time you have to choose. You can strengthen all three while you're still employed — which is exactly when it's easiest to do.

**17.** Tell us in the comments: in your field, does the AI story you hear at work match what you actually see happening? And here's the question to carry with you. When the next headline arrives, will you be reading it — or will it be deciding for you?
